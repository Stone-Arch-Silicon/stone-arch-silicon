// About

Four electrons in the outer shell. That's the whole reason any of this works.

Silicon sits exactly halfway between conductor and insulator, which is why you can talk it into being either one. Learning to do that on purpose, at scale, is chip design — and almost nobody gets to try it as an undergrad. SASi exists so that you can.

⌁

The whole flow

RTL through synthesis, place-and-route, and signoff — taught hands-on, in order, with nothing skipped because it's hard.

⚙

Tools you'll use again

Cadence, Synopsys, and Siemens flows on our own 20-node cluster. Not a browser demo — the real thing, with real licenses.

▣

A path to the fab

We work on open PDKs and shuttle programs, so a design that clears signoff has somewhere real to go.

◈

People who review your work

Design reviews, verification reviews, and teammates who will tell you your timing doesn't close. That's the part you can't self-teach.

// Ways In

You do not need to have taken the class.

Our constitution lists four ways to qualify as an active member, and they're deliberately equivalent. We care whether you can do the work, not which course number you have on a transcript.

Path 01

Coursework

EE 2301, or an equivalent digital logic, computer engineering, or HDL course from anywhere.

Path 02

ASIC 101

Our own curriculum. Start at zero, finish the assessment, you're in. Built for people with no background.

Start lesson 01 →
Path 03

Portfolio

Synthesizable HDL, a testbench, an FPGA build, an open-source hardware contribution, relevant work or research.

Path 04

A conversation

A short competency review with someone on the board. If you know the material, we'd rather just talk to you.

Open to every UMN student — undergraduate, graduate, and professional. EE, CompE, CS, Physics, Math, and anyone else who wants in.

// The ASIC Network

We're not doing this alone.

Chip design is hard to break into and much harder to figure out by yourself. So we helped start a network of university ASIC and VLSI clubs that work in the open and share what they learn.

What membership actually gets you

Shared documentation and tooling notes, so nobody re-derives the same install headache from scratch at six different schools. Biweekly calls where clubs trade progress, flows, and the gotchas that cost them a week.

Open tutorial tracks for both analog and digital design, written by students at member chapters and free for anyone to work through. Plus a Discord where the whole network talks day to day, and the ASIC Network Hackathon.

// Sponsor

Silicon is expensive. Undergrads are not.

Shuttle slots, tool licenses, cluster hardware, and getting members to conferences all cost real money. If your company hires people who can take a design from RTL to GDSII, this is where some of them come from.

▦

Tapeouts

Shuttle slots and mask costs. The difference between a design that passes signoff and a design that exists.

⬡

Tools & compute

Licenses and cluster hardware. We maintain our own 20 nodes, and nodes get old.

≡

ASIC 101

Our free curriculum, open to anyone. Keeping it written and current takes member hours we'd like to pay for.

◇

Getting out there

Conference travel, competitions, cleanroom training, and speakers worth flying in.

How it works

Tell us what you'd like to support and what kind of relationship you're after. We'll come back with a proposal, and the College of Science & Engineering handles the paperwork from there.

In-kind counts too. Tool licenses, PDK access, wafer or shuttle slots, and hardware are often more useful to us than cash.
